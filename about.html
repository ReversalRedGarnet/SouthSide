What this atlas is for

It maps conservation sites in East Are'are, West Are'are and Small Malaita and shows, for each, what is known, how we know it, and what is still missing. It is built so that students and community members can add careful, sourced, permitted information over time.

What counts as a site

Conservation in Solomon Islands mostly happens on customary land, so a legal designation is only one kind of site. The atlas separates three kinds so that nobody mistakes a community commitment for a legal protection:

  • Legally declared protected area (solid circle on the map): registered under the Protected Areas Act 2010. Kira is the only one found so far in these three constituencies.
  • Community-managed area (ring): a defined area with community rules, such as a taboo or no-take zone, without national registration.
  • Community conservation group (ring with a centre dot): an association or initiative that works on conservation, where the area it looks after is not yet defined in published sources.

A dashed circle around a marker means the location is only known roughly; a marker on its own is an exact point.

Key Biodiversity Areas are scientific designations, not community sites, and are noted only for context.

Considered but outside the area

These came up in research but are not in East Are'are, West Are'are or Small Malaita, so they are not profiled:

  • Manaoba Locally Managed Marine Area, Lau Lagoon (north-east Malaita) [S07].
  • Kwainaa'isi, Kafarumu and Aifasu, Kwaio highlands, mapped by the Baru Conservation Alliance, about 5.7 km² combined [S11].
  • Key Biodiversity Areas listed for Malaita Province: Langa Langa Lagoon, Lau Lagoon and Ndai [S10].

Leads we are following

These came up in desk research. They are leads and organisations to approach, not sites. None has been located, and nobody involved has been asked whether they want to be listed.

  • Mai-Maasina Green Belt: a network of about thirty tribal communities across Are'are, Kwaio and Small Malaita [S23] [S24]. This is the network to approach first, before recording any of its member communities as sites. Its anchor site is reported as Waihau in West Are'are, with rangers trained from Waihau, Hi'i, Wa'ariai, Horoia, Aparoro, Ahetaha, Raripaina and Kira [S23]. See also the Mai-Maasina Green Belt conservation village profile.
  • Communities named as beneficiaries in a Mai-Maasina Green Belt project report: Waisurione, Gala, Ahetaha, Suupaura, AruAru and Waisisi [S24].
  • Harurarumu Forest Community Conservation Area, Malaita: a 2014 Rufford-funded project, led by the person who now coordinates the Mai-Maasina Green Belt. Location and constituency not known, and no source has been recorded yet.
  • Hunanawa (Maramasike Passage) and Liwe (Small Malaita): fishing communities in a 2017 catch study. No conservation site is reported there [S26].
  • Ta'arutona: described as having a reef marine management area. Constituency not confirmed [S26].
  • A Maramasike Passage mangrove biodiversity assessment (MESCAL project), published by the Ministry of Environment in 2021. Only its objectives were found, not the report itself.

Ask them before assuming a gap.

Sensitive information

  • Sacred, taboo or tribal-secret places, and locations of threatened species, are generalised or withheld. Where a source mentions taboo sites (as at Puriasi), this atlas does not expand on them.
  • True coordinates of sensitive places must never be committed to the public repository, because version history keeps them forever. Keep them in a private register.
  • Named individuals in sources (officials, coordinators, project officers) are not republished unless they agree to be listed. Photographs and videos are credited as the original credits them, including photographers named by a news outlet or organisation, because credit is required.
  • Raw interviews, genealogies and community-held documents stay out of the public repository, and so do photograph and video files.
  • Photographs: location data is removed; no identifiable children; identifiable adults only with recorded consent; no place shown for sensitive sites.
  • "Visitor access" defaults to Unknown - ask first. Publishing a place can increase visitor pressure, so a pin is never an invitation.

About the boundaries

  • Date. The boundaries come from the 2009 census. The file was published in 2018, and OCHA reports reviewing it in October 2024 with no update needed. The Electoral Commission states that constituencies were last redrawn in 1997 and that a 2009 proposal to add seats was rejected [S31], so these boundaries should still be current.
  • Accuracy. The coastline matches the OpenStreetMap base map closely. The inland lines between constituencies are straight, schematic lines, not surveyed boundaries.
  • Changes made here. Only the three constituencies were kept, converted to latitude and longitude, and simplified (to about 44 m, with islets under about half a hectare removed) so the page stays light. Downloaded 19 October 2026.
  • Meaning. These are electoral units. They say nothing about customary land, tribal boundaries or who may speak for a place.

Corrections and removal

In a real release this section would name a monitored contact. For this prototype there is none yet. The intended process: anyone can ask for a correction, and a custodian can ask for any profile or detail to be hidden at once while the request is considered. Hidden does not mean deleted from history, which is why sensitive details are never committed in the first place.

Known limitations of this prototype

  • Only one site has a map point, and it is a bay-level point rather than a boundary.
  • Constituency boundaries date from the 2009 census, and their inland lines are approximate. See About the boundaries.
  • Photographs and videos are in private testing. Only material posted publicly by organisations is used, never an individual’s own account. Each item is credited, links to its original, and some can be loaded in the page with a click. All are labelled “Test use only” and will not appear in a public release without review.
  • No Solomon Pijin or Are'are-language summaries. These should be written by local reviewers.
  • Most sources are news articles, NGO or funder pages and grant reports in which organisations describe their own work, and some are several years old.
